\chapter{Results and Comparison}
\label{ch:ch7_results}

softening, from what it does not. The engine has been modelled, written,
verified in simulation against official vectors and synthesized on a real
standard-cell library; it has not yet run on the \ac{FPGA}. Every number
below therefore carries its provenance tag, and the entropy campaign ---
the one result that requires silicon on the bench --- is stated as a
protocol to execute, not as data.

Three readings, and the third is the one that matters.

\paragraph{The numerical floor is far enough below the signal.} With the
noise source switched off the measurement returns 1.38~fs, which is the
resolution of the simulation itself, not physics. It sits about 870 times
below the smallest jitter to be measured, so the floor does not
contaminate the sweep. Reaching it required care: the first attempt
returned 0.33~ps, of the same order as the quantity under study, and the
cause was the ring's start-up transient being included in the statistics.
Discarding the first twenty cycles removed it. A measurement floor that
coincides with the signal is indistinguishable from a result, which is
precisely why the floor was characterized before the sweep and not after.

\paragraph{The scaling law holds where it should.} Thermal jitter
accumulates as the square root of the noise density, so quadrupling the
density must double $\sigma$. From $1\times10^{-20}$ to
$4\times10^{-20}$ the measurement gives a factor of 2.01 against a
predicted 2.00. That agreement is the evidence that what is being measured
is the injected noise and not an artefact of the solver.

\paragraph{And it breaks at the top of the sweep, visibly.} At
$1.6\times10^{-19}$, sixteen times the reference density, $\sigma$ grows by
a factor of 21.3 where the law predicts 4.0, and the mean period drops from
230.4 to 227.8~ps. A zero-mean noise source cannot shift a mean period.
The point is therefore outside the regime the model describes --- the
perturbation is large enough to change the operating point of the
inverters --- and it is reported rather than dropped, because the boundary
of validity of a model is itself a result. Only the two central points are
used for anything downstream.

\section{The ring with the transistors of an open PDK}
\label{sec:ihp_ring}

The same five-stage ring was simulated with the low-voltage transistors of
the IHP SG13G2 open \ac{PDK} ($W_n = 0.5~\mu$m, $W_p = 1.2~\mu$m,
$L = 0.13~\mu$m, 1.2~V, 27~$^\circ$C, typical corner, 1~fF per node as a
pre-layout estimate of wiring), with the noise density measured on the
same devices (Section~\ref{sec:spice}) injected at every node.
Table~\ref{tab:ihp_ring} gives the result over 400~ns, about 1070
periods per point. The simulator draws a new noise realization on every
run and cannot be made to repeat one (in ngspice~47 the Gaussian
generator behind the transient noise source is seeded from the process
identifier, and none of its seed options reaches it), so the uncertainty
of the jitter was obtained by repetition: the \ac{PDK} thermal point was
simulated eight times.

The resolution is stated first: 0.68~fs, 262 times below the jitter that
the device noise produces, and with an exponent of 0.42 that marks it as
numerical noise rather than a random walk. The eight thermal realizations
spread by 3.3\,\% in $\sigma$ (169.0 to 186.5~fs), so a single run carries
that uncertainty and the mean carries 1.2\,\%. Quadrupling the density
multiplies the jitter by $1.99 \pm 0.06$ against the expected 2, and the
mean period does not move, so the bench stays in the linear regime that
the placeholder bench left at its highest point. The accumulation
exponent averages 0.99: white noise, as the stochastic model assumes. As
an independent check, Abidi's closed form for ring
oscillators~\cite{abidi_2006_ring}, evaluated with quantities measured at
the same bias point, predicts $\sigma/T = 1.2\times10^{-3}$; the
simulation gives 0.41 times that, the same order. The long-channel form of that formula, which goes through
the threshold voltage, does not apply here: at the switching point the
devices sit only 0.07~V above threshold.

With $\sigma/T = 4.83\times10^{-4}$ the divider for $Q \ge 0.2286$ is
$K_D = 981\,095$ ($958\,895$ to $1\,004\,347$ within one standard
deviation), a sampling interval of 359~$\mu$s with an identical
sampling ring, and 2.79~kbit/s per \ac{ERO}; if the independent jitter of
the sampling ring is also credited, $K_D$ halves and the rate doubles,
but that requires the two rings not to share noise through supply or
substrate, which only silicon can show. One design consequence was acted
on immediately: the divider counter had 20 bits, so the largest $K_D$ was
$2^{20} = 1\,048\,576$, a 7\,\% margin (4 to 9\,\%) over the requirement
in the typical corner at room temperature. It now has 24 bits (four flip-flops,
0.04~kGE), and the full-engine bench and its cross-check were re-run with
it.

\paragraph{Corners and temperature.} The thermal point was repeated in the
slow, typical and fast corners at $-40$, 27 and 125~$^\circ$C, with the
device noise recomputed at each point and four realizations per point
\simu{}. Cold is the worst case, not heat: $\sigma/T$ rises with
temperature in all three corners, because thermal noise follows $kT$
while the period changes little, so the required divider falls by a
factor of 2.5 to 3.5 from $-40$ to 125~$^\circ$C. The slow corner is worse
than the fast one, and the two combine worse than their product: the
slow corner at $-40~^\circ$C ($\sigma/T = 3.15\times10^{-4}$, period
438.6~ps) needs $K_D = 2\,306\,215$. There $2^{20}$ gives
$H_\infty = 0.78$ and $2^{21}$ only 0.970, so a fixed divider valid over
the whole range at the nominal 1.2~V is $2^{22}$, with
$H_\infty = 0.9995$ at the worst point and 0.51 to 0.82~kbit/s depending
on the corner. Supply is the third factor and the strongest. At
$-40~^\circ$C in the slow corner, lowering the supply by 10\,\% to
1.08~V lengthens the period by 34\,\% while $\sigma$ hardly moves, so
the required divider grows by a factor of 1.70 to $K_D = 3\,913\,109$
($3.74$ to $4.10\times10^6$ at $\pm1\sigma$); at 1.32~V it shrinks by a
factor of 0.79. The asymmetric corners at $-40~^\circ$C (sf, fs) need
1.32 and $1.55\times10^6$, close to tt (1.47) and below ss. At the worst point,
$2^{22}$ still gives $H_\infty = 0.9855$, but with a 7\,\% margin on
$K_D$, the same margin judged too thin for $2^{20}$ at the typical
point; $2^{23}$ gives 0.9999 and 0.20 to 0.41~kbit/s, about 1.9~s for the
384 bits of a seeding in the worst case. The divider register resets to
$2^{24}$, so a master that does not write it is already on the safe
side; a lower setting has to come from the coldest corner and the lowest
supply the product must work at.

\paragraph{Flicker noise.} Adding the $1/f$ noise of the \ac{PDK} leaves
400~ns unchanged: $\sigma$ rises by 3.8\,\%, within the spread between
realizations, and the separation $E[D^2] = a_1 M + a_2 M^2$, validated
beforehand on synthetic phases (thermal recovered to 0.1\,\% with and
without $1/f$), finds $a_2$ compatible with zero. Amplifying the $1/f$
source to make it visible does not work, and the reason is instructive:
the source is broadband, and amplified a thousandfold its density near
the oscillation frequency is already seven times the thermal floor, so
the ring converts it as white noise and the linear term grows by the
expected factor while the quadratic term does not scale. The answer comes
instead from the impulse sensitivity function of Hajimiri and
Lee~\cite{hajimiri_1998_general}, measured by injecting 1\,\% of the node
charge at 40 phases (linear to 0.1\,\% between 0.5 and 2\,\%). It is
nearly symmetric, $|\Gamma_{dc}/\Gamma_{rms}| \approx 0.03$--$0.05$, so
low-frequency noise injected uniformly over the period is barely
converted. But the transistors are not symmetric: measured separately,
the p-channel device has three times the $1/f$ noise of the n-channel
one, and each injects noise mainly while it conducts, during one of the
two opposite lobes. The two limits bracket the truth: with stationary
noise, as in the bench, the $1/f^3$ corner is 36~kHz and flicker equals
thermal jitter after 27\,000 periods; with each device confined to its
lobe, the corner is 5.3~MHz and the crossover 185 periods. In both cases,
at the operating divider flicker dominates the accumulated variance, by a
factor between 36 and 5\,300. Two consequences follow. The jitter that
sets the entropy bound must be measured at short accumulation, below
about a hundred periods for this ring, and separated from the quadratic
term --- inferring it from the total accumulated jitter at $K_D$ would
credit entropy that is not there. And the stationary bench may
overestimate the thermal variance by up to a factor of two, which would
double $K_D$; the 24-bit counter covers it, and the tool that settles it
is a cyclostationary transient-noise analysis, a concrete use of a
commercial simulator.

\section{Operating point: divider and throughput}
\label{sec:opoint}

The stochastic model fixes the target. For the PTG.2 thresholds of AIS
20/31 v3.0, the min-entropy criterion ($H_\infty \ge 0.98$) demands
$Q \ge 0.2286$, and is more demanding than the Shannon criterion
($H \ge 0.9998$, $Q \ge 0.2022$); the design therefore targets the former,
with $Q = 0.30$ as the working set-point, which yields
$H_\infty = 0.9951$. Two properties of the model were established rather
than assumed: the truncated series of equation~14
in~\cite{baudet_2011_security} \emph{overestimates} entropy and is thus
not a lower bound, so the exact wrapped-Gaussian computation is used
instead; and the first-harmonic derivation of min-entropy agrees with the
exact one to six digits over the whole range of interest.

Since $Q_{\mathrm{gen}} = K_D \cdot Q_{\mathrm{raw}}$, the divider follows
from the jitter that the campaign measures. Table~\ref{tab:opoint} gives
the operating points for the jitter range expected of this
technology~\cite{petura_2016_survey}, assuming RO2 at 500~MHz.

$Q \ge 0.2286$ \est{}.}

These bracket the published design point of Fischer and
Lubicz~\cite{fischer_2014_embedded} ($K_D \approx 430\,000$ for
$\sigma = 5.01$~ps on $T = 7.81$~ns), which is the sanity check that the
model is being applied as its authors intended.

The architectural consequence is stated plainly: a single \ac{ERO}
delivers kbit/s, not Mbit/s. That is the price of demonstrable entropy,
and it is affordable here because the engine's product is a 256-bit seed
for the \ac{DRBG}, not a bit stream. More throughput means more
independent rings, each with its own bound, not a faster ring.

The honest reading of that table has two halves.

On capability and assurance the commercial parts win, and it is not close.
Two of them carry CC EAL6+ evaluations, which represent an amount of
third-party scrutiny --- including physical attack resistance --- that no
academic project reproduces. They offer asymmetric cryptography, certified
provisioning flows and manufacturing support. This work offers symmetric
key generation and authentication, and carries no certification at all.

On what the thesis is actually about, the comparison is more nuanced than
``open versus closed'', and it pays to be exact. Of the three, only the
ATECC608B has its entropy source validated under SP~800-90B: the NIST
record names ring oscillators and states 0.5071~bit of entropy per
sample~\cite{nist_esv_e46}. That is a real, third-party figure, and it must
be credited. What no vendor publishes --- not Microchip either --- is the
stochastic model behind the figure, the jitter measurement that supports
it, or the circuit itself; the datasheet of one competitor claims
compliance without a validation record and the other does not mention
SP~800-90 at all. Their randomness is trusted because the vendor and the
evaluation scheme are trusted. The engine described here makes the opposite
trade: it has no certificate, and it can show where each bit of entropy
comes from, what physical measurement bounds it, and what the bound becomes
when the temperature or the divider changes. That is the same distinction
that the black-box study of the ESP32 generator in this work runs into from
the other side (Section~\ref{sec:esp32_90b}) --- a black box can be tested
statistically but cannot be bounded --- and it is the reason this engine
was built as a white box.

Which of the two is preferable depends on the question being asked. For
shipping a product, the certified part. For knowing what the randomness in
that product is worth, only the open one answers.

\section{The ESP32 reference}
\label{sec:esp32_ref}

Chapter~\ref{ch:esp32} characterizes the generator of the ESP32 under
four operating conditions and on three chips; the comparison of O8 takes
its figures from there.

Three consequences follow for O8. First, the figure this thesis carries
forward as the ESP32 reference is the non-IID one, 0.81 to 0.92 bit per
bit with a mean of 0.85 over the thirty-six captures of conditions A--D,
because it is what an evaluator would credit without granting the
IID assumption, with the restart test as the reminder that for
condition~D it is a statistical figure and not physical entropy; the engine's raw bits, when the campaign of
Section~\ref{sec:protocol} produces them, must be run through the same
battery at the same $N$, and compared estimator by estimator, since the
same conservative bias will act on them; the calibration of the IID tests
at that length applies to them unchanged. Second, the Shannon figure of
0.99999 bit per bit obtained in the first campaign and the
min-entropy figures above are not in contradiction --- $H_\infty \le H$
always --- but they are different currencies: crediting 256 bits of
entropy costs 256 output bits in the first and up to 316 in the second,
and only the second is the one key generation may use. Third, and
independent of any number, the measurement is of a black box: the source
and post-processing behind \texttt{esp\_fill\_random} are undocumented,
and the SP 800-90B estimators are designed for raw noise, not for an
output that may already be conditioned; the restart test shows how far
that can go, a stream of pseudo-random output that every estimator scores
like noise. What the ESP32 cannot supply,
whatever the estimators return, is the bound that Section~\ref{sec:opoint}
derives for the engine from a physical measurement. That asymmetry is the
comparison.

\section{Uncertainty budget}
\label{sec:uncertainty}

Five quantities carry the conclusions, and each has a different and
declared kind of uncertainty.

\begin{description}
\item[Simulated jitter.] Resolution floor 1.38~fs \simu{}, about 870 times
below the smallest quantity measured. The scaling check against the
expected square-root law agrees to 0.5\,\% in the valid regime, and the
regime boundary is documented in Section~\ref{sec:spice_results}. On the
open \ac{PDK} the floor is 0.68~fs, 262 times below the jitter, whose
run-to-run spread is 3.3\,\% (1.2\,\% for the mean of eight), and the
noise density is no longer assumed but taken from the device models.
What remains approximate is how that noise is applied: stationary
injection may overestimate the thermal variance by up to a factor of two
against a cyclostationary model, and pre-layout wiring is a 1~fF
estimate. Process corners and temperature were covered (ss, tt, ff at
$-40$ to 125~$^\circ$C), and at $-40~^\circ$C also the asymmetric
corners and a $\pm10$\,\% supply; the other corners at low supply and
device mismatch were not. The figure is therefore a simulated value with a
factor-of-two band, not the jitter of a fabricated device.
\item[Recovered $Q$.] The estimator reproduces a known $Q$ with a bias
below 2\,\% and a dispersion of 4.0\,\% over eight seeds with 20\,000
blocks \simu{}. Without the corrections applied here a plain straight-line
fit biases the result by $-18$\,\%, so the correction is not cosmetic.
\item[Area.] A lower bound \simu{}: cells only, flattened, without routing,
fill or clock tree. Published growth factors from cell area to core area
on this library are $\approx 2.35$ for an \ac{AES}, and the optimization
savings of Chapter~\ref{ch:ch6_asic} are \est{} and explicitly not claimed
as measured.
\item[Min-entropy of the ESP32 reference.] Measured \meas{} on thirty-eight
captures of $10^6$ to $8.4\times10^6$ bits, nine per condition, three on
each of three chips of the same model identified by MAC address. The
IID-track figure is at the resolution ceiling of the MCV estimator for
that $N$ (0.9963 at $10^6$ bits), so it is a floor on the ceiling rather
than a measurement of the source, and the IID verdicts are read against
the measured false-failure rate of each test at that length, not against
the nominal one. The non-IID figure is set by estimators whose
conservative bias on nearly uniform data exceeds the spread between
conditions; an analysis of variance finds no condition effect
($p \ge 0.045$ on every estimator) and no chip effect ($p \ge 0.032$,
against 0.0045 after correction). Three devices show that the reference
does not hinge on one of them; they do not bound the spread across a
production population. The restart test was run on two chips, in
condition~D, with the Wi-Fi started but not associated, and on the second
chip with each source switched on and off in turn; Bluetooth and an
associated radio were not tested. It bounds the entropy that the stock bootloader injects
only from below: no repeat in 1000 restarts means more than about
$1.7\times10^5$ equally likely starting states, some 17 bits, at 95\,\%
confidence. The size of the generator's internal state is not documented.
\item[Min-entropy of the built device.] Not yet bounded. The model, the
estimator and the cutoffs are ready; the number requires the campaign of
Section~\ref{sec:protocol}. No value is offered here, and none should be
inferred from the simulated jitter.
\end{description}

\section{The pending campaign}
\label{sec:protocol}

The one result that silicon must supply is the min-entropy of the rings as
built, and the procedure is fixed in advance so that it cannot be tuned
after seeing the data.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Confirm the rings oscillate.} Read the frequency of each
ring with the embedded counter before trusting any entropy measurement. A
ring removed by the synthesizer, or two rings that have locked to each
other, would otherwise be indistinguishable from a working source.
\item \textbf{Measure the jitter} with $K_D = 1$ by the method of Fischer
and Lubicz~\cite{fischer_2014_embedded}, sweeping the pair distance $M$
and fitting the linear region, with at least three rings so that the
global component separates from the local one~\cite{lubicz_2024_jitter}.
Fit $\sigma^2(t) = at + bt^2$ and keep only the thermal term.
\item \textbf{Fix $K_D$} from the measured jitter for $Q \ge 0.2286$ with
margin, and recompute the health-test cutoffs for the measured $H_\infty$
rather than the assumed 0.98, using \texttt{cortes\_salud.py}.
\item \textbf{Collect raw bits} at that operating point and run the
\ac{NIST} SP 800-90B estimators, contrasting the empirical estimate with
the model's bound. Agreement supports the model; disagreement is a result
about the model's hypotheses, and either way it is reported.
\item \textbf{Repeat across placement} --- rings in separated regions
versus adjacent ones --- to quantify the coupling that the literature
warns about, which is the measurement the architecture of this engine was
chosen to make possible.
\end{enumerate}

Until step 4 closes, the engine's claim is precise and limited: the chain
from physical noise to key material is designed, implemented and verified
against the standards, and the bound it will produce is computable from a
measurement that has not yet been taken.
