\chapter{Results and Comparison}
\label{ch:ch7_results}

This chapter collects what the work establishes and separates it, without
softening, from what it does not. The engine has been modelled, written,
verified in simulation against official vectors, synthesized on a real
standard-cell library and run complete on an \ac{FPGA} with an ESP32 on
its bus. Every number below carries its provenance tag. The entropy
campaign on the \ac{FPGA} had been written down as a protocol before the
board existed; Section~\ref{sec:campaign} reports it step by step and
declares where the protocol was not followed. Two different rings appear
and must not be mixed: the integrated circuit's, which exists only in
simulation, and the \ac{FPGA}'s, which was measured.

Three readings, and the third is the one that matters.

\paragraph{The numerical floor is far enough below the signal.} With the
noise source switched off the measurement returns 1.38~fs, which is the
resolution of the simulation itself, not physics. It sits about 870 times
below the smallest jitter to be measured, so the floor does not
contaminate the sweep. Reaching it required care: the first attempt
returned 0.33~ps, of the same order as the quantity under study, and the
cause was the ring's start-up transient being included in the statistics.
Discarding the first twenty cycles removed it. A measurement floor that
coincides with the signal is indistinguishable from a result, which is
precisely why the floor was characterized before the sweep and not after.

\paragraph{The scaling law holds where it should.} Thermal jitter
accumulates as the square root of the noise density, so quadrupling the
density must double $\sigma$. From $1\times10^{-20}$ to
$4\times10^{-20}$ the measurement gives a factor of 2.01 against a
predicted 2.00. That agreement is the evidence that what is being measured
is the injected noise and not an artefact of the solver.

\paragraph{And it breaks at the top of the sweep, visibly.} At
$1.6\times10^{-19}$, sixteen times the reference density, $\sigma$ grows by
a factor of 21.3 where the law predicts 4.0, and the mean period drops from
230.4 to 227.8~ps. A zero-mean noise source cannot shift a mean period.
The point is therefore outside the regime the model describes --- the
perturbation is large enough to change the operating point of the
inverters --- and it is reported rather than dropped, because the boundary
of validity of a model is itself a result. Only the two central points are
used for anything downstream.

\section{The ring with the transistors of an open PDK}
\label{sec:ihp_ring}

The same five-stage ring was simulated with the low-voltage transistors of
the IHP SG13G2 open \ac{PDK} ($W_n = 0.5~\mu$m, $W_p = 1.2~\mu$m,
$L = 0.13~\mu$m, 1.2~V, 27~$^\circ$C, typical corner, 1~fF per node as a
pre-layout estimate of wiring), with the noise density measured on the
same devices (Section~\ref{sec:spice}) injected at every node.
Table~\ref{tab:ihp_ring} gives the result over 400~ns, about 1070
periods per point. The simulator draws a new noise realization on every
run and cannot be made to repeat one (in ngspice~47 the Gaussian
generator behind the transient noise source is seeded from the process
identifier, and none of its seed options reaches it), so the uncertainty
of the jitter was obtained by repetition: the \ac{PDK} thermal point was
simulated eight times.

The resolution is stated first: 0.68~fs, 262 times below the jitter that
the device noise produces, and with an exponent of 0.42 that marks it as
numerical noise rather than a random walk. The eight thermal realizations
spread by 3.3\,\% in $\sigma$ (169.0 to 186.5~fs), so a single run carries
that uncertainty and the mean carries 1.2\,\%. Quadrupling the density
multiplies the jitter by $1.99 \pm 0.06$ against the expected 2, and the
mean period does not move, so the bench stays in the linear regime that
the placeholder bench left at its highest point. The accumulation
exponent averages 0.99: white noise, as the stochastic model assumes. As
an independent check, Abidi's closed form for ring
oscillators~\cite{abidi_2006_ring}, evaluated with quantities measured at
the same bias point, predicts $\sigma/T = 1.2\times10^{-3}$; the
simulation gives 0.41 times that, the same order. The long-channel form of that formula, which goes through
the threshold voltage, does not apply here: at the switching point the
devices sit only 0.07~V above threshold.

With $\sigma/T = 4.83\times10^{-4}$ the divider for $Q \ge 0.2286$ is
$K_D = 981\,095$ ($958\,895$ to $1\,004\,347$ within one standard
deviation), a sampling interval of 359~$\mu$s with an identical
sampling ring, and 2.79~kbit/s per \ac{ERO}; if the independent jitter of
the sampling ring is also credited, $K_D$ halves and the rate doubles,
but that requires the two rings not to share noise through supply or
substrate, which only silicon can show. One design consequence was acted
on immediately: the divider counter had 20 bits, so the largest $K_D$ was
$2^{20} = 1\,048\,576$, a 7\,\% margin (4 to 9\,\%) over the requirement
in the typical corner at room temperature. It was widened to 24 bits at
once (four flip-flops, 0.04~kGE) and later to 26, when the ring was
lengthened (Section~\ref{sec:ihp_ring11}).

The rest of this section keeps the five-stage ring as the
characterization of the technology: corners, supply, mismatch and flicker
noise were studied on it. The final design does not use it as it stands.
The sampled ring has eleven stages and the sampling ring thirteen, for
the reason given in Section~\ref{sec:ff_mc}, and the divider and
throughput of the integrated circuit are those of
Section~\ref{sec:ihp_ring11}, not the ones derived below.

\paragraph{Corners and temperature.} The thermal point was repeated in the
slow, typical and fast corners at $-40$, 27 and 125~$^\circ$C, with the
device noise recomputed at each point and four realizations per point
\simu{}. Cold is the worst case, not heat: $\sigma/T$ rises with
temperature in all three corners, because thermal noise follows $kT$
while the period changes little, so the required divider falls by a
factor of 2.5 to 3.5 from $-40$ to 125~$^\circ$C. The slow corner is worse
than the fast one, and the two combine worse than their product: the
slow corner at $-40~^\circ$C ($\sigma/T = 3.15\times10^{-4}$, period
438.6~ps) needs $K_D = 2\,306\,215$. There $2^{20}$ gives
$H_\infty = 0.78$ and $2^{21}$ only 0.970, so a fixed divider valid over
the whole range at the nominal 1.2~V is $2^{22}$, with
$H_\infty = 0.9995$ at the worst point and 0.51 to 0.82~kbit/s depending
on the corner. Supply is the third factor and the strongest. At
$-40~^\circ$C in the slow corner, lowering the supply by 10\,\% to
1.08~V lengthens the period by 34\,\% while $\sigma$ hardly moves, so
the required divider grows by a factor of 1.65 to $K_D = 3\,808\,445$
($3.71$ to $3.91\times10^6$ at $\pm1\sigma$, eight realizations); at
1.32~V it shrinks by a factor of 0.79. The asymmetric corners at
$-40~^\circ$C (sf, fs) need 1.32 and $1.55\times10^6$, close to tt (1.47)
and below ss; at 1.08~V, tt, sf and fs need 1.93, 1.57 and
$1.74\times10^6$, half of ss. At the worst point, $2^{22}$ still gives
$H_\infty = 0.9873$, but with a 10\,\% margin on $K_D$, close to the
7\,\% judged too thin for $2^{20}$ at the typical point; $2^{23}$ gives 0.9999 and 0.20 to 0.41~kbit/s, about 2.6~s for the
512 bits of a seeding in the worst case. Device mismatch, drawn per
transistor from the \ac{PDK} statistical models with a new noise
realization in every run, spreads the period by 1.3\,\% over 48 dies at
the typical point and by 1.6\,\% over 15 at the worst point, but leaves
$\sigma/T$ where it was: its die-to-die spread (2.6 and 3.2\,\%) is no
larger than the run-to-run spread of the noise alone. A sixteenth
worst-point run was discarded because its crossing count does not fit in
the simulated time, most likely double crossings of a slow edge; the
crossing detector has since gained hysteresis and records any such event
with its waveform. Eight further dies run with it at the same point show
no double crossing, and their crossings fit in 392.4--393.5~ns, like the
other fifteen \simu{}. Over the 23 dies, four need more than $2^{22}$; the
worst needs $K_D = 4.49\times10^6$ ($H_\infty = 0.973$ at $2^{22}$), and
$2^{23}$ covers every one ($H_\infty \ge 0.9996$). For a five-stage ring
the fixed divider would therefore be $2^{23}$: it has to come from the
coldest corner and the lowest supply the product must work at, not from
the typical point.

\paragraph{Flicker noise.} Adding the $1/f$ noise of the \ac{PDK} leaves
400~ns unchanged: $\sigma$ rises by 3.8\,\%, within the spread between
realizations, and the separation $E[D^2] = a_1 M + a_2 M^2$, validated
beforehand on synthetic phases (thermal recovered to 0.1\,\% with and
without $1/f$), finds $a_2$ compatible with zero. Amplifying the $1/f$
source to make it visible does not work, and the reason is instructive:
the source is broadband, and amplified a thousandfold its density near
the oscillation frequency is already seven times the thermal floor, so
the ring converts it as white noise and the linear term grows by the
expected factor while the quadratic term does not scale. The answer comes
instead from the impulse sensitivity function of Hajimiri and
Lee~\cite{hajimiri_1998_general}, measured by injecting 1\,\% of the node
charge at 40 phases (linear to 0.1\,\% between 0.5 and 2\,\%). It is
nearly symmetric, $|\Gamma_{dc}/\Gamma_{rms}| \approx 0.03$--$0.05$, so
low-frequency noise injected uniformly over the period is barely
converted. But the transistors are not symmetric: measured separately,
the p-channel device has three times the $1/f$ noise of the n-channel
one, and each injects noise mainly while it conducts, during one of the
two opposite lobes. The two limits bracket the truth: with stationary
noise, as in the bench, the $1/f^3$ corner is 36~kHz and flicker equals
thermal jitter after 27\,000 periods; with each device confined to its
lobe, the corner is 5.3~MHz and the crossover 185 periods. In both cases,
at the operating divider flicker dominates the accumulated variance, by a
factor between 36 and 5\,300. Two consequences follow. The jitter that
sets the entropy bound must be measured at short accumulation, below
about a hundred periods for this ring, and separated from the quadratic
term --- inferring it from the total accumulated jitter at $K_D$ would
credit entropy that is not there. And the stationary bench may
overestimate the thermal variance by up to a factor of two, which would
double $K_D$. The final design keeps a factor of two between the divider
the simulation asks for and its minimum divider
(Section~\ref{sec:ihp_ring11}); that is one margin, and this factor
would consume it, not a second one on top. The tool that settles it is a
cyclostationary transient-noise analysis, a concrete use of a commercial
simulator.

\paragraph{Duty cycle.} Once the phase is random, the probability of a
one is the fraction of the period that RO1 spends above the threshold of
the sampling flip-flop. Whatever the jitter, the duty cycle $D$ bounds the
min-entropy of a directly sampled bit at $-\log_2 \max(D, 1-D)$. Simulated
without noise on the same five-stage ring, it depends on the node, with
the same pattern at every corner. At $V_{DD}/2$, two nodes sit at
0.517--0.528 and the other three at 0.486--0.508. Mismatch spreads it to
0.483--0.535 over eight dies at the typical point and eight at the worst
point. A threshold 100~mV away from $V_{DD}/2$ moves it by about 0.019,
because the edges are slow against a 270--600~ps period \simu{}. The bound
falls to 0.90 at $V_{DD}/2$ and to 0.85 with that threshold uncertainty.
The \ac{FPGA} shows the same effect with another ring and another
flip-flop \meas{}: in streams of $10^6$ bits the fraction of ones is
0.4951--0.4966 or 0.5054--0.5064 with the quiet wrapper, depending on the
implementation, which bounds the bit at 0.982--0.990, and 0.513--0.519
with the display and the frequency meter attached (0.946--0.962). The
sign of the bias changes from one implementation to the next
(Section~\ref{sec:campaign}). The duty cycle of the eleven-stage ring was
not simulated on its own; the Monte Carlo of the next subsection contains
it, because it samples the ring through the real flip-flop.

\subsection{The sampling flip-flop and the length of the ring}
\label{sec:ff_mc}

The sampling flip-flop biases the bit more than the ring does. With the
real \texttt{sg13g2\_\allowbreak dfrbpq\_1} of the library on node~5, behind a
\texttt{sg13g2\_\allowbreak buf\_1} (without it the flip-flop's input capacitance
pulls the ring, about 2--3~ps of period modulation), and the probability
of a one weighted over the sampling phase, tt and ss stay within 0.01 of
the ideal threshold. At 125~$^\circ$C with mismatch they do not: over four
dies each, $P(1)$ is 0.372--0.461 at ff and 0.399--0.437 at sf, while the
ideal threshold on the same rings gives 0.49--0.51 \simu{}. The flip-flop
does not capture a rising and a falling input at the same instant: the
difference, expressed as an equivalent delay, is 26--30~ps at sf and
13--15~ps at ff, and it stays there when the ring is lengthened to nine
and eleven stages, so the bias falls roughly as $1/T$.

Those eight dies already ruled out the first revision of the declared
entropy, $\Hdecl = 0.80$: five of them fell below it and the worst gave
0.672. The value was lowered to $\Hdecl = 0.60$, with cut-offs and a
512-bit seed computed for it (Section~\ref{sec:health}), and declared
provisional, because eight dies say little about a tail. The criterion
that would close it was written down before simulating anything else: a
Monte Carlo of 24 dies per corner; the declarable entropy is
$-\log_2\max(p, 1-p)$ evaluated at $p = \mu - 3\sigma$ of $P(1)$, and
the minimum over the corners is the figure; if it falls below 0.60 the
circuit is corrected, and $\Hdecl$ is not lowered again.

Table~\ref{tab:ff_mc} gives both Monte Carlo campaigns. With the
five-stage ring the declarable entropy is \textbf{0.532}, below 0.60, in
the asymmetric corner at 125~$^\circ$C and low supply; even the worst
single die of that corner, at 0.596, is below. By the criterion, the
circuit was corrected. Of the options that the $1/T$ law offers, a longer
sampled ring was preferred to RO1 followed by a divide-by-two, whose
toggle flip-flop at 2.7~GHz would bring an asymmetry of its own that was
not simulated: RO1 now has eleven stages, 2.2 times the period. The
Monte Carlo was repeated with it, on the same four corners plus the fast
corner at $-40~^\circ$C and 1.32~V, where the period is shortest: 24 dies
in each of five corners, 120 in all. The declarable entropy is
\textbf{0.757} \simu{}, again set by sf at 125~$^\circ$C and 1.08~V, so
$\Hdecl = 0.60$ stands, the cut-offs do not change and the circuit is not
touched again. With $\sigma$ at the upper end of its 95\,\% confidence
interval that corner still gives 0.704.

How the 24 dies would be read was also fixed before the last batch
finished. In some dies the sampling phases leave part of the period
uncovered, and the phase-weighted $P(1)$ is then uncertain by about the
width of that gap over $\sqrt{6}$. The official figure uses every die; a
sensitivity reading drops the dies with a gap above 0.025 of a period
(error up to 0.01) and never decides alone. It gives 0.789 with 22 dies
in the limiting corner, so both readings pass. The same sensitivity
reading on the five-stage campaign gives 0.530 against 0.532: dropping
those dies would not have changed that decision either.

Three limits are stated. These are simulations without noise, with the
statistical models of an open \ac{PDK}. The critical corners were run in
two batches of 11 and 13 dies, and in sf at 1.2~V the two batches differ
in their mean ($0.4700$ against $0.4592$, Welch $p = 0.004$); the cause
was not established --- the candidate is the period of the sampling
clock, which each batch takes from a previous simulation of another die
--- and the official figure pools both batches, so that variation is
inside its $\sigma$. And one die of tt at 125~$^\circ$C comes out biased
the other way, $P(1) = 0.5278$, the only one of the 120 above one half;
it inflates the $\sigma$ of that corner, which makes its figure more
conservative, and its cause was not examined.

\subsection{Eleven-stage ring: divider and throughput of the integrated
circuit}
\label{sec:ihp_ring11}

A longer RO1 has less relative jitter per period, so the divider was
recomputed. The eleven-stage ring was simulated at the worst point found
above, ss at $-40~^\circ$C and 1.08~V, without mismatch, eight
realizations of 900~ns (about 700 periods each) \simu{}: $T = 1240.9$~ps,
$\sigma = 214.9 \pm 4.0$~fs, no double crossings, and a variance per RO1
period $(\sigma/T)^2 = (3.00 \pm 0.26)\times10^{-8}$ (95\,\% confidence
interval of the mean), with $2.65\times10^{-8}$ in the lowest realization. The
five-stage ring gave about $6.0\times10^{-8}$ at the same point, and
$6.0 \times 5/11 = 2.7$: the $1/N$ law of the number of stages holds. The
spread between the eight realizations, 10.5\,\%, is larger than the
5.6\,\% that the same estimator gives on white jitter with as many
crossings; the cause was not identified. It does not reach the divider,
which is computed from the lowest realization and keeps a factor of 2.3
before \kd{24} is lost.

The sampling ring, RO2, has thirteen stages rather than eleven, because
two equal rings tend to lock to each other, and it feeds a divide-by-16
prescaler, because a flip-flop of the library needs about as long to
toggle as the whole period of a five-stage ring \est{}
(Section~\ref{sec:ro_silicon}); the divider still counts periods of RO2. Its period, simulated without noise,
is 1.1754 times that of RO1 at this corner (1458.6 against 1241.0~ps) and
1.174 to 1.175 at the other three corners simulated, a little under
$13/11$ because the load of the last stage does not scale with the number
of stages. Equation~\eqref{eq:q2} then gives
$q_2 = 1.1754 \times 3.00\times10^{-8} = 3.5\times10^{-8}$ on average;
the jitter of RO2 itself is not credited. Table~\ref{tab:ihp_e11} gives
the min-entropy of the model for each divider.

register takes at reset, is \kd{25} (\texttt{CFG\_KD} $= 25$), and the
counter reaches $2^{26}$. That factor of two over what the simulation
asks for is deliberate: it stands for what the simulation does not
contain --- layout parasitics, supply noise and deterministic components
of the phase. On the \ac{FPGA}, where both could be measured, the white
part of the phase noise turned out more than ten times smaller than the
apparent one (Section~\ref{sec:campaign}).

The worst-die column is conservative, and it is not a Monte Carlo of the
eleven-stage ring: mismatch was not simulated with eleven stages. The
factor of 1.175 comes from the five-stage ring. It is a ratio of extremes
dominated by the estimation error of a single realization of 650 to 700
periods, not a pure measure of mismatch --- on those dies the die-to-die
spread of $\sigma/T$ was no larger than the run-to-run spread --- and it
is applied here on top of the lowest of eight realizations, so that error
is counted twice. For \kd{24} to fall below $H_\infty = 0.98$ the
variance per period would have to be 2.6 times below the nominal mean of
$3.00\times10^{-8}$, and 5.2 times below it for the minimum divider
\kd{25} \est{}.

At \kd{25} the bit rate follows from the period of RO2, which was
simulated without noise or mismatch and with 1~fF per node from 594.4~ps
(ff, $-40~^\circ$C, 1.32~V) to 1458.6~ps (ss, $-40~^\circ$C, 1.08~V): one
bit every 20 to 49~ms, \textbf{20 to 50~bit/s} \est{}. The start-up test
(one window of 1024 bits) takes 20 to 50~s, and a seeding, 512 bits plus
the wait for the window that holds the last of them, 10 to 75~s. The
fastest corner is the cold fast one, not the hot one. Two things move
these figures by a few per cent: the real load on RO2 (on RO1 the
flip-flop with its buffer lengthens the period by about 3\,\%) and
mismatch (1.3\,\% of period spread on the five-stage ring). This is two
orders of magnitude below the kbit/s of the first estimates, and it is
what the bias of the flip-flop, the worst corner and the margin cost
together; the engine seeds a deterministic generator and can afford it.

\section{Operating point: divider and throughput}
\label{sec:opoint}

The stochastic model fixes the target. For the PTG.2 thresholds of AIS
20/31 v3.0, the min-entropy criterion ($H_\infty \ge 0.98$) demands
$Q \ge 0.2286$, and is more demanding than the Shannon criterion
($H \ge 0.9998$, $Q \ge 0.2022$); the design therefore targets the former,
with $Q = 0.30$ as the working set-point, which yields
$H_\infty = 0.9951$. Two properties of the model were established rather
than assumed: the truncated series of equation~14
in~\cite{baudet_2011_security} \emph{overestimates} entropy and is thus
not a lower bound, so the exact wrapped-Gaussian computation is used
instead; and the first-harmonic derivation of min-entropy agrees with the
exact one to six digits over the whole range of interest.

the jitter. Table~\ref{tab:opoint} gives the operating points estimated

($q_2 = Q_{\mathrm{raw}}$) and RO2 at 500~MHz.

price of demonstrable entropy, and it is affordable here because the
engine's product is a 512-bit seed for the \ac{DRBG}, not a bit stream.
More throughput means more independent rings, each with its own bound,
not a faster ring.

The honest reading of that table has two halves.

On capability and assurance the commercial parts win, and it is not close.
Two of them carry CC EAL6+ evaluations, which represent an amount of
third-party scrutiny --- including physical attack resistance --- that no
academic project reproduces. They offer asymmetric cryptography, certified
provisioning flows and manufacturing support. This work offers symmetric
key generation and authentication, and carries no certification at all.

On what the thesis is actually about, the comparison is more nuanced than
``open versus closed'', and it pays to be exact. Of the three, only the
ATECC608B has its entropy source validated under SP~800-90B: the NIST
record names ring oscillators and states 0.5071~bit of entropy per
sample~\cite{nist_esv_e46}. That is a real, third-party figure, and it must
be credited. What no vendor publishes --- not Microchip either --- is the
stochastic model behind the figure, the jitter measurement that supports
it, or the circuit itself; the datasheet of one competitor claims
compliance without a validation record and the other does not mention
SP~800-90 at all. Their randomness is trusted because the vendor and the
evaluation scheme are trusted. The engine described here makes the opposite
trade: it has no certificate, and it can show where each bit of entropy
comes from, what physical quantity bounds it, and what the bound becomes
when the temperature or the divider changes. That is the same distinction
that the black-box study of the ESP32 generator in this work runs into from
the other side (Section~\ref{sec:esp32_90b}) --- a black box can be tested
statistically but cannot be bounded --- and it is the reason this engine
was built as a white box.

Which of the two is preferable depends on the question being asked. For
shipping a product, the certified part. For knowing what the randomness in
that product is worth, only the open one answers.

\section{The ESP32 reference}
\label{sec:esp32_ref}

Chapter~\ref{ch:esp32} characterizes the generator of the ESP32 under
four operating conditions and on three chips; the comparison of O8 takes
its figures from there.

Three consequences follow for O8. First, the figure this thesis carries
forward as the ESP32 reference is the non-IID one, 0.81 to 0.92 bit per
bit with a mean of 0.85 over the thirty-six captures of conditions A--D,
because it is what an evaluator would credit without granting the
IID assumption, with the restart test as the reminder that for
condition~D it is a statistical figure and not physical entropy; the engine's raw bits
were run through the same battery at the same $N$ and are compared
estimator by estimator in Section~\ref{sec:o8}, since the same
conservative bias acts on them. Second, the Shannon figure of
0.99999 bit per bit obtained in the first campaign and the
min-entropy figures above are not in contradiction --- $H_\infty \le H$
always --- but they are different currencies: crediting 256 bits of
entropy costs 256 output bits in the first and up to 317 in the second,
and only the second is the one key generation may use. Third, and
independent of any number, the measurement is of a black box: the source
and post-processing behind \texttt{esp\_fill\_random} are undocumented,
and the SP 800-90B estimators are designed for raw noise, not for an
output that may already be conditioned; the restart test shows how far
that can go, a stream of pseudo-random output that every estimator scores
like noise. What the ESP32 cannot supply,
whatever the estimators return, is the bound that Section~\ref{sec:opoint}
derives for the engine from a physical quantity, the jitter of its
rings. That asymmetry is the
comparison.

\section{The simulated source under the same battery}
\label{sec:sim_90b}

A source whose entropy is known by construction tells what the battery
can and cannot see, so the stochastic model was run through exactly the
pipeline that produced the ESP32 figures: same binaries, options and
length. The bits come from the model of the \ac{ERO} driven by the jitter
of the five-stage open-\ac{PDK} ring (Section~\ref{sec:ihp_ring}) at
three points --- tt at 27~$^\circ$C, ss at $-40~^\circ$C and 1.08~V, and
the worst chip with mismatch at that point --- for dividers from $2^{14}$
to $2^{24}$, three seeds each, $10^6$ bits per sequence \simu{}. The
study predates the eleven-stage ring and was not repeated with it. It
does not need to be: the simulated bits depend only on the product
$Q = K_D\,q_2$, so a ring with another $q_2$ shifts the divider axis and
changes nothing else. With the final rings the axis moves by about 0.8 in
$\log_2 K_D$, and the integrated circuit's \kd{25} lies beyond the
points $2^{23}$ and $2^{24}$ simulated here, where the model gives
$H_\infty \ge 0.9996$ and the battery is saturated. As a reference, uniform independent bits of the same
length were run through the same battery. Figure~\ref{fig:sim_90b} sets
the exact min-entropy of the model against the non-IID estimate.

Four readings follow. First, at $10^6$ bits the battery saturates: uniform
bits score $0.839 \pm 0.021$, and every point where the model gives
$H_\infty \ge 0.97$ scores between 0.82 and 0.87, always limited by the
compression estimator. Above that ceiling the battery tells nothing
apart, so the ESP32, at 0.85, and the simulated source at $2^{23}$, the
divider a five-stage ring would use, at 0.84 to 0.85 in the three cases,
are indistinguishable at this length;
the figures must not be compared as if they differed. Second, at low
entropy the battery \emph{overestimates}: at $2^{14}$ the model gives
$H_\infty \approx 0$ and the battery 0.07 to 0.11, and up to $2^{19}$ at
the worst point 0.10 to 0.32 against 0 to 0.25. The source is then nearly
deterministic, its phase advancing by a known step with little noise, and
generic predictors do not exploit that structure fully; the model does,
because it grants the attacker the previous phase. A divider chosen too
small would not have been caught by the battery alone, which is the case
AIS~31 makes for a stochastic model, here in the design's own numbers.
Third, the IID track accepts data that is not full-entropy: at $2^{21}$
the worst chip ($H_\infty = 0.79$) and the ss point (0.85) pass the three
IID tests in five sequences of six, and the IID credit, the byte
most-common-value estimate, would be 0.86 to 0.90 bit per bit, above the
model; at $2^{20}$ ($H_\infty \approx 0.5$) the $\chi^2$ test rejects
every sequence. This supports carrying the non-IID figure for the ESP32
and applying the same rule to the engine. Fourth, between those regimes,
for $H_\infty$ from about 0.5 to 0.95, the estimate sits below the model,
as a conservative estimator should.

A longer record raises the ceiling, but for every source alike. At the
lengths of the two long radio-off ESP32 captures, $3.52 \times 10^6$ and
$8.38 \times 10^6$ bits, the divider $2^{23}$ was rerun in the three cases
with uniform bits as reference, three seeds each \simu{}. The twelve
sequences with $H_\infty \approx 1$ average 0.881 (0.860 to 0.925) and
0.912 (0.889 to 0.935); the ESP32 captures score 0.867 and 0.911
\meas{}, inside those spreads at both lengths, and the ideal bits score
$0.869 \pm 0.003$ and $0.926 \pm 0.016$. The limiting estimator is still
compression, or the t-tuple in three sequences, whose bias shrinks only
slowly with $N$. At $8.38 \times 10^6$ bits the IID track in bytes
accepts all twelve sequences, as it does the ESP32 capture. The
comparison of objective O8 therefore cannot rest on a difference between
SP~800-90B figures: above its ceiling the battery only says that both
sources are above it. What separates them is what stands behind the
figure, a physical model and a divider derived from it on the engine side
and none on the ESP32 side, which without boot noise repeats sequences
across chips (Table~\ref{tab:esp32_restart}).

These bits contain only white Gaussian jitter. Flicker noise, coupling
between rings, a shared supply and slow drift are absent, and they are
what the battery is least able to see. The board confirmed both the
saturation and the blindness \meas{}: on the \ac{FPGA} the battery gives
the same figure at \kd{16}, where the model with the measured white floor
gives $H_\infty \approx 0.47$, and at \kd{19}, where it gives 0.9996
(Table~\ref{tab:fpga_90b}), and it scores a deterministic modulation of
the phase as entropy. Three seeds make the spreads indicative only. The reading in Spanish, with
the byte and IID tables, is in \nolinkurl{docs/fuente_simulada_90b.md}.

\section{The FPGA campaign}
\label{sec:campaign}

The campaign was run on the Basys~3 of Section~\ref{sec:bringup}, with
the whole engine in the \ac{FPGA} and the ESP32 verifier as bus master,
collecting raw bits in test mode. On this board the sampling ring RO2 has
eleven look-up-table stages and the sampled ring RO1 thirteen. Unless
stated otherwise the data come from the quiet wrapper --- no display and
no frequency meter, the configuration closest to the integrated circuit
--- with the placement of every ring stage fixed. Appendix~\ref{app:fpga}
sets these results beside those of the first prototype board.

The procedure had been fixed in five steps before the board existed, so
that it could not be tuned after seeing the data: confirm that the rings
oscillate; measure the jitter at $K_D = 1$ by the method of Fischer and
Lubicz~\cite{fischer_2014_embedded}, keeping only the linear term of a
fit $\sigma^2(t) = at + bt^2$; fix $K_D$ from that jitter and recompute
the health-test cut-offs for the measured entropy; collect $10^6$
consecutive raw bits at that divider and contrast the SP~800-90B
estimate with the model's bound; and repeat with the rings in separated
and in adjacent regions. The steps are reported in that order. Three of
them were not carried out as written, and each deviation is declared
where it occurs.

\paragraph{Step 1: the rings oscillate.} The frequency meter of the
normal wrapper reads 207.2~MHz for RO2 and 167.2~MHz for RO1 \meas{}
($\pm0.1$~MHz of reading), and the duration of a stream gives 205~MHz for
RO2 in the quiet wrapper (Table~\ref{tab:ringfreq}). The build script
lists every ring cell and net after routing, so a ring removed or merged
by the tools cannot pass for a working source.

\paragraph{Step 2: jitter.} The capture buffer gives chunks of 4096
consecutive bits at any divider, and each capture is 2000 chunks at
$K_D = 4$, preceded in the first bitstreams by 2000 at $K_D = 1$, with no
CRC error in any of them. Three
estimators of $q_2$ were applied to the same bits: the variance estimator
of Section~\ref{sec:estimator}; a fit of the autocorrelation of the bits
against the pair distance, $c(M)$, with the model of the \ac{ERO} and one
mean phase step for the whole capture; and the same fit with one phase
step per chunk, which removes the drift between chunks.
Table~\ref{tab:fpga_q} gives the results.

The first three rows do not measure the same thing, and none of them
measures thermal jitter. Each autocorrelation estimator repeats within a
few per cent, so the factor of three to four between them is systematic:
it depends on how much of the shape of $c(M)$ each one attributes to
jitter. The model does not describe that shape. On the first bitstreams,
with the accumulation exponent left free, the fit returns $b = 1.6$ to
2.1, where thermal jitter gives one, and the residuals of the joint fit
are 5 to 250 times the binomial noise, growing with the range of $M$. This is the negative
result that Chapter~\ref{ch:ch1_intro} anticipated, and the first
prototype board had shown the same on another family and another process
node. The logic attached to the rings also matters: with the ring stages
in the same look-up tables, the wrapper with the frequency meter gives a
joint-fit $q_2$ 4.4 times smaller than the quiet one, each value repeated
within 1\,\% in a second capture. What hangs from the rings changes
their apparent relative phase noise; the placement of the stages does
not explain it.

The spectrum of the instantaneous frequency shows what the fits were
absorbing. Each chunk is demodulated at its own mean phase step, the
phase is taken over blocks of 16 samples and the spectra are averaged
over the chunks. A narrow line stands at about 39.4~kHz, more than a
hundred times above the floor in the quiet wrapper. It is fixed in time,
not in ring periods: it sits at 38 to 40~kHz with RO2 at 130 and at
205~MHz. It is not phase-locked to the start of a capture. It is not the
\ac{I2C} traffic during the capture: a firmware command that keeps the
bus silent while the buffer fills leaves the line where it was (1.13
against 1.13 in the units of the spectrum, two captures each way). A
load of 4000 flip-flops toggling at 100~MHz elsewhere in the device does
not change its amplitude and moves it by 0.9\,\%. Its power is fifteen
times smaller with the frequency meter attached. And a carrier near
480~kHz with sidebands at $\pm39.5$~kHz is present in every bitstream. A
switching regulator of the board is a hypothesis compatible with all of
this; it was not verified, and the origin of the line remains
unidentified. What is established is that a deterministic modulation of
external origin enters the relative phase of the rings \meas{}, which is
what explains $b \approx 2$ and the disagreement between estimators.

Away from the line the spectrum is flat, and that floor is the white
part of the phase noise. It was converted into $q_2$ by calibration
against simulated bits of known $q_2$ with a modulation of the measured
frequency and amplitude \simu{}. The validation on simulated bits set the
limits of the method before the figure was used: without modulation no
line appears and the floor grows linearly with $q_2$; but the floor also
rises with the spread of the phase step between chunks, with the bias of
the bits and with the harmonics of the line, and it depends on the phase
step itself. The floor therefore contains white jitter \emph{plus}
components of the estimator, and the $q_2$ read from it errs upwards,
the non-conservative side. For the quiet wrapper with fixed placement,
three calibration models give 0.80 to $0.88\times10^{-6}$ and the bands
between 100 and 400~kHz give 0.59 to $1.13\times10^{-6}$: \textbf{$q_2 =
0.6$ to $1.1\times10^{-6}$} \meas{}, calibration \simu{}, that is, a
jitter of 4 to 5~ps per 4.9~ns period, of the order of the 2 to 4~ps
reported for rings in this technology~\cite{petura_2016_survey}. For the
wrapper with the frequency meter the mean phase step is different and the
calibration does not determine $q_2$.

This is the first declared deviation. The protocol asked for a
polynomial fit of the accumulated variance and for keeping its linear
term; that fit does not describe these data, and the separation of
global from local jitter with a third ring~\cite{lubicz_2024_jitter} is
not available to an engine with two. The white floor comes
instead from a spectral estimator introduced after the data had been
seen, on the proposal of an independent review. It was validated on
simulated bits with and without a known modulation, its bias is in a
known direction, and what is carried to the divider is the lower edge of
its range.

With the lower edge of the floor, $q_2 = 0.6\times10^{-6}$, \kd{19}
(\texttt{CFG\_KD} $= 19$) gives $H_\infty = 0.996$; it stays above 0.99
while $q_2 \ge 5.0\times10^{-7}$ and above $\Hdecl = 0.60$ while
$q_2 \ge 1.3\times10^{-7}$. The \ac{FPGA} build therefore takes \kd{19}
as both its reset value and its minimum divider: about 390~bit/s with RO2
at 205~MHz. A further factor of two on $q_2$ would ask for $2^{20}$ and
was not taken; it is the margin this operating point does not have. The
final bitstream, which differs from the one above in the timing
constraint of RO2 and in the minimum divider, was measured again and
gives 0.95 to $1.35\times10^{-6}$ by band, above the edge used. The
divider this replaced is worth recording. The joint fit of
Table~\ref{tab:fpga_q} had suggested $K_D \ge 2^{16}$ with a factor of
two of margin, and most of the first campaign was run there; with the
white floor that divider gives $H_\infty \approx 0.47$. A divider derived
from a jitter figure that includes a deterministic modulation is not
conservative.

The second deviation is in this step. The cut-offs of the health tests
were not recomputed from an entropy measured on the \ac{FPGA}. They are
those of $\Hdecl = 0.60$, which the sampling flip-flop of the integrated
circuit fixes (Section~\ref{sec:ff_mc}); the \ac{FPGA}, whose bias
bounds the bit near 0.98, runs with the cut-offs of the product.

serial link of the ESP32 does not keep up, and the dividers $2^{8}$ to
$2^{12}$ were recorded as 245 chunks of 4096 bits, which are not

The protocol said that agreement between the battery and the model would
support the model and that disagreement would be a result about its
hypotheses. The result is a disagreement, and it is about the battery as
much as about the model. From \kd{12} to \kd{19} the estimate is flat,
0.815 to 0.876, the saturation band that uniform bits give at this
length (Section~\ref{sec:sim_90b}), while the model with the measured
white floor goes from nearly zero to 0.9996 over the same dividers. The
battery does not see the lack of entropy when the phase is deterministic
and modulated; contrasted with the simulated source, the figures at
$2^{10}$ and $2^{11}$ are those of a $q_2$ of 4 to $8\times10^{-5}$,
fifty to a hundred times the white floor. It does see the lowest
dividers, where the bits are plainly correlated. The same campaign
without fixed placement, with RO2 at 130~MHz, gives the same picture:
0.125, 0.558 and 0.786 at $2^{8}$, $2^{10}$ and $2^{11}$, and 0.819 to

from this table. It comes from the white floor, and the battery only
fails to contradict it.

Two properties of the bits do not depend on the divider. The fraction of
ones is 0.5054 to 0.5064 at every divider of this bitstream, 0.4951 to
0.4963 without fixed placement and 0.4959 in the final bitstream: a bias
of about half a per cent that is 7 to 13 standard deviations from one
half in $10^6$ bits, and whose sign changes with the implementation. It
comes from the sampling path, not from the jitter. And there is no lag-1
correlation in the streams ($|z| \le 2.1$, one of the eight streams of
the table above two), with the flip-flop sampling on every edge of RO2
(Section~\ref{sec:health}).

A long record does not change the reading. At 8\,378\,368 consecutive
bits, the length of the longest ESP32 capture, the estimate is 0.932
without fixed placement and 0.880 with it, both at \kd{16}, against 0.907
to 0.935 for ideal bits of that length; the fraction of ones is 0.4955
and 0.5054 and $\rho_1$ is within 1.1 standard deviations of zero. These
two records were taken before the white floor was measured, at a divider
where the model gives $H_\infty \approx 0.47$. No long record exists at
\kd{19}. They are therefore a statement about the battery, which scores
them like ideal bits, and not about the source.

\paragraph{Health tests on the board.} With the cut-offs of
$\Hdecl = 0.60$ (51 and 766), the divider was lowered on purpose and the
alarms watched for about 40~s at each setting. Without fixed placement
the repetition test fires at $2^{8}$, where $\Hnb = 0.125$, and nothing
fires at $2^{6}$ or at $2^{10}$, where $\Hnb = 0.558$ is already below
the declared value. With fixed placement nothing fires at $2^{6}$,
$2^{8}$, $2^{10}$ or $2^{11}$: at $2^{8}$ the bits alternate
($\rho_1 = -0.54$, $\Hnb = 0.186$), the longest run in the recorded
chunks is 8 against a cut-off of 51 and the largest proportion count 546
against 766 \meas{}. A repetition test looks for a stuck source and a
proportion test for a biased one; neither can see anticorrelated bits.
The health tests do not protect the low-jitter regime, and whether they
happen to fire there depends on the implementation. That is the
measurement behind the minimum divider enforced in hardware
(Section~\ref{sec:health}): it is the only protection against a host
that writes a low divider. At the operating divider, with the measured
bias and independent bits, the estimated false-alarm rate is below
$10^{-5}$ per year \est{}.

\paragraph{Step 5: placement.} This is the third deviation: the
comparison between rings in separated and in adjacent regions was not
made. The rings were kept in their two regions throughout. What was
varied instead is the placement of the stages inside each region (left
to the tool, or one look-up table per stage, which also changes the
frequency of RO2 from 130 to 207~MHz), the logic attached to the rings
(quiet wrapper against display and frequency meter), and the load on the
core supply. Those comparisons answered a different question from the
one asked: the apparent phase noise depends on what hangs from the rings
and a modulation enters from outside, but how much two adjacent rings
couple was not measured.

\paragraph{What the campaign establishes.} The rings of the \ac{FPGA}
have a white phase-noise floor of 0.6 to $1.1\times10^{-6}$ per RO2
period, measured inside the device with the caveat that the estimator
errs upwards; with its lower edge, \kd{19} gives $H_\infty \ge 0.99$ in
the model. Everything else the bits contain --- a modulation near
39.4~kHz of unidentified origin, a bias of half a per cent that follows
the implementation --- is not entropy, and neither the SP~800-90B battery
nor the health tests tell it apart from entropy. The stochastic model
describes the white part and not the shape of the measured
autocorrelation, so the bound is a bound on the white part only.

\section{Uncertainty budget}
\label{sec:uncertainty}

Seven quantities carry the conclusions, and each has a different and
declared kind of uncertainty.

\begin{description}
\item[Simulated jitter.] Resolution floor 1.38~fs \simu{}, about 870 times
below the smallest quantity measured. The scaling check against the
expected square-root law agrees to 0.5\,\% in the valid regime, and the
regime boundary is documented in Section~\ref{sec:spice_results}. On the
open \ac{PDK} the floor is 0.68~fs, 262 times below the jitter, whose
run-to-run spread is 3.3\,\% (1.2\,\% for the mean of eight), and the
noise density is no longer assumed but taken from the device models.
What remains approximate is how that noise is applied: stationary
injection may overestimate the thermal variance by up to a factor of two
against a cyclostationary model, and pre-layout wiring is a 1~fF
estimate. Process corners and temperature were covered (ss, tt, ff at
$-40$ to 125~$^\circ$C), and at $-40~^\circ$C also the asymmetric
corners, a $\pm10$\,\% supply and the tt, sf and fs corners at low
supply, and device mismatch at the typical and the worst point; ff at
low supply and low supply when hot were not. The figure is therefore a simulated value with a
factor-of-two band, not the jitter of a fabricated device.
\item[Recovered $Q$.] The estimator reproduces a known $Q$ with a bias
below 2\,\% and a dispersion of 4.0\,\% over eight seeds with 20\,000
blocks \simu{}. Without the corrections applied here a plain straight-line
fit biases the result by $-18$\,\%, so the correction is not cosmetic.
\item[Area.] A lower bound \simu{}: cells only, flattened, without routing,
fill or clock tree. Published growth factors from cell area to core area
on this library are $\approx 2.35$ for an \ac{AES}. Of the optimization
savings of Chapter~\ref{ch:ch6_asic}, two are synthesized and the third
is \est{} and explicitly not claimed as measured. Renaming alone moves
the figure of the logic optimizer by about 0.2\,\%.
\item[Min-entropy of the ESP32 reference.] Measured \meas{} on thirty-eight
captures of $10^6$ to $8.4\times10^6$ bits, nine per condition, three on
each of three chips of the same model identified by MAC address. The
IID-track figure is at the resolution ceiling of the MCV estimator for
that $N$ (0.9963 at $10^6$ bits), so it is a floor on the ceiling rather
than a measurement of the source, and the IID verdicts are read against
the measured false-failure rate of each test at that length, not against
the nominal one. The non-IID figure is set by estimators whose
conservative bias on nearly uniform data exceeds the spread between
conditions; an analysis of variance finds no condition effect
($p \ge 0.045$ on every estimator) and no chip effect ($p \ge 0.032$,
against 0.0045 after correction). Three devices show that the reference
does not hinge on one of them; they do not bound the spread across a
production population. The restart test was run on two chips, in
condition~D, with the Wi-Fi started but not associated, and on the second
chip with each source switched on and off in turn; Bluetooth and an
associated radio were not tested. It bounds the entropy that the stock bootloader injects
only from below: no repeat in 1000 restarts means more than about
$1.7\times10^5$ equally likely starting states, some 17 bits, at 95\,\%
confidence. The size of the generator's internal state is not documented.
\item[Bias of the sampling flip-flop.] Simulated without noise \simu{}
on 24 dies in each of five corners. The declarable figure, 0.757, uses
$\mu - 3\sigma$ of the worst corner; with $\sigma$ at the upper end of
its 95\,\% interval it is 0.704, and without the dies whose sampling
phases leave a gap, 0.789. Two batches of one corner differ in their mean
for a reason that was not established, and the statistical models are
those of an open \ac{PDK}. The margin to $\Hdecl = 0.60$ is what absorbs
all of that.
\item[Min-entropy on the \ac{FPGA}.] Bounded through the
model from a measured quantity \meas{}, with a calibration \simu{}: a
white floor $q_2 = 0.6$ to $1.1\times10^{-6}$, which gives
$H_\infty \ge 0.99$ at \kd{19} with its lower edge. The estimator of the
floor errs upwards, by an amount that depends on the phase step and was
not bounded, and the lower edge of the range is the only allowance made
for it; a factor of two on $q_2$ would bring the bound to 0.974. One
wrapper on one board at room temperature; temperature and supply were
not varied, and the origin of the modulation near 39.4~kHz is unknown.
The SP~800-90B figures of the same bits, 0.83 to 0.86, are at the
ceiling of the battery for $10^6$ bits and bound nothing.
\emergencystretch=1em
\item[Min-entropy on the integrated circuit.] Simulated
and then estimated, not measured. At the worst corner the variance per
period of the eleven-stage ring is $3.00\times10^{-8}$ \simu{}, with a
95\,\% interval of $\pm0.26\times10^{-8}$ over eight realizations, and
the divider \est{} is computed from the lowest
realization with a worst-die factor borrowed from the five-stage ring.
Mismatch with eleven stages, corners other than the worst one, the
jitter of the thirteen-stage ring, the timing of the prescaled domain in
a slow library and everything a layout adds were not simulated. The
factor of two between \kd{24} and the minimum divider \kd{25} is the
allowance for it, and the \ac{FPGA} is the warning of how much a
simulation of white noise can leave out. No value measured on the
\ac{FPGA} transfers to the integrated circuit: it is a different ring.
\end{description}
