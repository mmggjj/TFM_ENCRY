\label{ch:ch6_asic}

The engine is designed to end up as a dedicated integrated circuit that
serves keys to a microcontroller, and the FPGA is its verification
vehicle, not its destination. This chapter takes the same VHDL that
passes the testbenches of Chapter~\ref{ch:ch5_verification}, synthesizes
it on an open standard-cell library, and states what a fabrication run
would cost and what would have to change in the entropy source. A
tape-out is explicitly out of scope of this thesis --- not for cost or
area, as will be seen, but for schedule --- and everything here is
labelled as what it is: a lower bound on area from logic synthesis, with
the growth to core area estimated from a range of utilization, not from
a placed and routed design.

\section{Standard-cell synthesis flow}
\label{sec:synthflow}

The flow is deliberately reproducible with free tools. Yosys reads the
VHDL through the GHDL front end, synthesizes each block flattened, maps
flip-flops and logic onto the liberty file of the target library and
reports cell count and area. The library is the open \ac{PDK} of IHP,
SG13G2, a 130~nm BiCMOS process with a maintained standard-cell library,
compiled SRAM macros, I/O cells and OpenROAD support, chosen because it is
the one open \ac{PDK} that is alive, European and reachable through the
same Europractice membership the university already holds. The gate
equivalent is read from the liberty file itself rather than from memory:
$1~\mathrm{GE} = $ area of \texttt{sg13g2\_nand2\_1} $= 7.2576~\mu\mathrm{m}^2$.
No clock constraint is applied: Yosys does not use one for area, so the
figures do not depend on a target frequency. The ring oscillators are not
synthesized; they are the one block designed at transistor level
(Chapter~\ref{ch:ch3_entropy}).

\section{Area per block in gate equivalents}
\label{sec:area}

Table~\ref{tab:synth} gives the result for each block, all synthesized in
one run on the final \ac{RTL}. The whole engine is \textbf{95.5~kGE}, and
the digital
engine --- everything except the capture buffer --- is about
\textbf{48.7~kGE}, the difference between the last row and the buffer's
($95.46 - 46.81$). Synthesized on their own, the authenticator and the
generator each carry their own one-round-per-cycle encryption core,
15.8~kGE apiece, and that is how their rows in the table are measured; in
the whole engine a single 32-bit core serves both, because the sequencer
never lets them run at the same time (Section~\ref{sec:opt}), so the rows
do not add up to the engine's figure. Roughly, from the rows: the shared
32-bit AES
6.6~kGE, the authenticator without its core 7.5, the generator without it
23.8, health tests 1.2, sampler, crossing and resets 0.4, \ac{I2C} 1.0,
and the remainder, about 8~kGE, of top-level sequencer, registers and
core arbitration.
The capture buffer is reported for completeness but must not be read as
part of the product: its 46.8~kGE are a 4096-bit memory mapped onto
flip-flops because the flow has no SRAM macros. On the \ac{FPGA} the
implementation tool maps it onto distributed RAM, 158 look-up tables and
44 flip-flops in the implemented design, with no block RAM; in silicon it
would be a compiled SRAM or,
more sensibly, a much smaller buffer, because it is a test path that is
disabled at production.

The sampler row is the block with its default 24-bit counter; inside the
engine the counter has 22 bits and sits behind the four toggle flip-flops
of the divide-by-16 prescaler of RO2, which belong to the top level
(Section~\ref{sec:ro_silicon}).

The numbers are internally consistent, and that consistency is how an
error in the flow was caught: before flattening, the report returned for
the authenticator and for the generator the area of their AES
sub-module alone. An authenticator weighing exactly as much as a bare AES
is impossible, so the report, not the design, was wrong. After
flattening, the authenticator adds to the AES 647 flip-flops (sub-keys,
chaining state, key) and 7.5~kGE, and the generator adds 2221 flip-flops
and 23.8~kGE, about two thirds of which are its registers \est{} (a
flip-flop of this library is 6.75 to 7.25~GE depending on the cell; the
cells the mapper chose were not counted).

The figure moved with the design, and each step was synthesized against
the previous one in the same run, because the logic optimizer alone
shifts the total by about 0.2\,\% when only signal names change, and
between runs of different days the same variant has moved by about
0.5\,\% (the engine with the one-round core: 104.60, 104.81 and
105.24~kGE in three runs of 19 and 22 November). At
register-map version~3 --- zeroization, a request counter that saturates
instead of wrapping, the capture exclusion rules and the reset
synchronizers --- the engine was 115.7~kGE with two encryption cores;
sharing one core took it to 101.6 and the 32-bit core to 92.5
(Section~\ref{sec:opt}). The 512-bit seed (Section~\ref{sec:health})
added 256 flip-flops, in the seed collector and in the generator's
entropy register, and 2.3~kGE. The thirteen-stage RO2 with its prescaler
and the wider divider added 0.5\,\% in the run in which they were
introduced (95.09 to 95.53~kGE, five flip-flops more), which is the size
of that noise: in the run of the table the engine with the earlier
generics comes out at 95.60~kGE, above the final one, so the change is
not measurable in area. Refusing a divider outside the admitted range
cost nothing measurable either: 95.46~kGE in the run of the table, with
the same 8\,425 flip-flops. The October runs
read the \ac{VHDL} through GHDL's Verilog netlist output and the
\texttt{pip}-installable build of Yosys, the table through the GHDL
plug-in; on the unchanged AES core the two paths agreed within 0.9\,\%
when they were compared in October, and the core's figure in the table,
15.80~kGE, is 1.6\,\% below the 16.06 of that month: differences under
about 2\,\% between runs of different dates and tool paths are not
design changes.

\section{Optimizations and their expected savings}
\label{sec:opt}

The synthesis is naive on purpose --- the \ac{RTL} was written to be
verifiable, not small --- and it points at three optimizations, in order
of return on effort:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{One shared AES core --- done.} The top-level sequencer
already serializes commands: the generator and the authenticator never
run at the same time. The core now lives in the top level and is handed
to whichever block raises its start pulse; a property in the system
bench checks that the two never do so in the same cycle. Measured
saving, in the run of October in which it was introduced:
\textbf{14.1~kGE} (115.7 to 101.6), a little under the 15.8
estimated, because the arbitration costs about half a kGE and the clear
input that the review required on the core and on the generator costs
another. A generic keeps the two-core arrangement available.
\item \textbf{Compact S-box datapath --- done.} Twenty independent S-box
tables synthesize to 16~kGE for the one-round core; area-optimized
designs in the literature range from 2.4~kGE for an 8-bit datapath at 226
cycles per block~\cite{moradi2011pushing} to about 5~kGE for 32-bit
datapaths. Speed is irrelevant here, so a second core was written with a
32-bit datapath: four S-boxes shared between SubBytes and the key
schedule's SubWord, one MixColumns, ShiftRows applied as a wiring
permutation of the state register at the start of each round, one column
per cycle, 51 cycles per block instead of 11. It passes the same official
vectors, the thousand-block chain and the zeroization test, and the bench
requires its output to equal the one-round core's on every block. Measured
when it was introduced, in October:
6.67~kGE against 16.06 for the core (a saving of 9.4, against
the 10 estimated, the difference being the column multiplexers and the
extra phase register), and 92.5 against 101.8~kGE for the whole
engine in the same synthesis run. In the run of Table~\ref{tab:synth}:
\textbf{6.64~kGE against 15.80} for the core and \textbf{95.46 against
105.24~kGE} for the engine, a saving of 9.8; over the four runs of
November in which both variants were synthesized the saving goes from
9.1 to 9.8~kGE. The one-round core remains
available through a generic.
\item \textbf{Fewer registers in the generator.} Of its 2483 flip-flops,
the captured entropy and nonce (512), the seed material (256), the output
(256) and the temporaries of the derivation function can be reduced by
consuming the seed as a stream and sharing temporaries; roughly 800
flip-flops, about 4~kGE.
\end{enumerate}

The two measured optimizations took the digital engine, in the October
runs, from 69~kGE (two one-round cores) to 46; with the 512-bit seed and
the changes to the source that followed it stands at 48.7~kGE, and the
third optimization would leave it at
\textbf{$\approx$45~kGE} \est{}. That is above the 13 to 25~kGE that this
work's own initial estimate, assembled from published block areas,
anticipated for an engine of this kind, and the table says why: the
generator without its core is 23.8~kGE, about two thirds of it
registers, and the
top-level sequencer and registers another 8; reaching that range
would mean rethinking the generator's storage rather than its cipher,
which this work leaves stated rather than done.

The step from cell area to core area is an estimate with a range, not a
result: no design was placed and routed. The utilization is taken
between 0.60, the ratio of routed to raw gate density published for the
standard cells of another open 130~nm process, not this
one~\cite{skywater_pdk_cells}, and 0.70, the figure assumed in the
first area budget of this work, that is, a core 1.4 to 1.7 times the cell area. With it, 45~kGE
(0.33~mm$^2$ of cells) is 0.47 to 0.54~mm$^2$ of core \est{}, below the
0.8~mm$^2$ that is the minimum charge on this process
(Section~\ref{sec:shuttle}). The first two savings are measurements; the
third is an estimate and is listed as future work, not claimed.

\section{What the ring oscillator needs in silicon}
\label{sec:ro_silicon}

The digital blocks port with the standard flow. The entropy source does
not, and the literature that has done the crossing --- notably a 2024
study that characterizes the same ring model on an Artix-7 and on a 28~nm
FD-SOI circuit~\cite{benea_2024_flicker} --- makes three points that the
chip design must absorb.

First, the ring is rebuilt from inverter cells rather than look-up tables,
and its jitter changes in value and in character: flicker noise gains
weight in newer nodes, and the study finds, against expectation, that
counting it as a legitimate source can raise throughput for the same
entropy. The stochastic model and the estimator of
Chapter~\ref{ch:ch3_entropy} carry over unchanged; the measured $\sigma$
does not. Second, process variation makes every die different, so the
frequency and jitter of each ring must be calibrated per chip; the
published all-digital designs that survive voltage and temperature
sweeps do it with a configurable ring and a tuning loop~\cite{yang_2016_jssc}.
Third, and most important for a security device, injecting a frequency on
the supply locks the rings and removes the jitter: a 500~mV signal on
the rail reduced the key space of a commercial secure microcontroller
from $2^{64}$ to about 3300~\cite{markettos_2009_frequency}, and a
contactless electromagnetic attack locked the fifty rings of an
\ac{FPGA} generator and controlled the bias of its
output~\cite{bayon_2012_contactless}. The same calibration
loop, together with supply filtering, is the published defence, and the
health tests of Section~\ref{sec:health} are the detector of last resort.
None of this is in the FPGA prototype; it is the work that a silicon
implementation adds.

Four requirements come from this work's own simulations and
measurements, and the \ac{RTL} already applies the first two.

\paragraph{Ring lengths.} RO1 has eleven stages and RO2 thirteen. RO1 is
long because the sampling flip-flop of the library does not capture a
rising and a falling input at the same instant, and that fixed asymmetry
biases the bit less the slower the sampled ring is: with five stages the
declarable entropy fell below the declared 0.60, with eleven it is 0.757
\simu{} (Section~\ref{sec:ff_mc}). RO2 is longer still, and deliberately
not equal to RO1, because two identical rings tend to lock to each
other. With these lengths and the minimum divider \kd{25}
(\texttt{CFG\_KD} $= 25$) the source delivers 20 to 50~bit/s over
corners \est{} (Section~\ref{sec:ihp_ring11}).

\paragraph{Timing of the ring domain.} The logic clocked by RO2 has to
close timing at the ring's own period, and a first estimate says that
it would not have with the original five-stage ring \est{} (logic
synthesis and the mapper's delay report on the typical library, 1.2~V
and 25~$^\circ$C): a 26-bit divider counter clocked directly by the
ring, as the design then had, takes 1.53~ns, and a
single flip-flop of the library needs about 0.37~ns to toggle, against
a period of 0.366~ns. With thirteen stages the simulated period is
0.905~ns in the typical corner at 27~$^\circ$C \simu{}, which leaves the
first flip-flop a margin of $\times2.4$ there, and a chain of
four toggle flip-flops divides RO2 by 16 before anything wider is
clocked, so the counter, now of 22 bits and not estimated again, runs
with a period sixteen times longer than the ring's (14.5~ns typical).
In the fastest corner simulated ($-40$~$^\circ$C, 1.32~V, fast
transistors) the period falls to 0.594~ns \simu{}: against the
typical-library 0.37~ns that would be $\times1.6$, a comparison across
corners that is only indicative, because no fast-corner library was
used for the flip-flop. This is an
estimate and it is open: the local \ac{PDK} has no slow-corner library,
so 1.08~V at 125~$^\circ$C is unchecked, and no static timing analysis
with constraints on the divided clock has been run.

\paragraph{Isolation of the sampled node.} The flip-flop must not hang
from the ring: without a buffer between them its input capacitance pulls
the node and modulates the period by 2 to 3~ps \simu{}. For the same
reason the monitor output that the \ac{FPGA} wrapper uses to measure the
ring frequencies must be left unloaded in a product.

\paragraph{Own supply or guard ring.} On the \ac{FPGA} the logic that
hangs from the rings changes the apparent phase noise by a factor of
4.4, with the ring stages at the same sites, and the relative phase of
the rings carries a narrow modulation at 38 to 40~kHz, fixed in time and
not tied to the ring frequency, whose origin was not identified \meas{}
(Section~\ref{sec:campaign}). In an \ac{FPGA} the rings cannot be
isolated; in an integrated circuit they can, with their own filtered
supply or a guard ring, which is also the defence against the injection
attacks above. That supply should be one branch shared by both rings,
not one per ring. Each ring alone is sensitive to its supply: its period
changes by 1.1 to 2.8\,\% per 10~mV across the corners \simu{}. The bit,
however, depends on the ratio of the two periods, and since both rings
use the same inverter a common disturbance largely cancels in it: 0.4\,\%
of a single ring's sensitivity remains at the nominal die, and at most
2 to 3\,\% (mean plus two standard deviations over 8 mismatch draws per
ring, typical and slow corners) \simu{}. Separate supplies would lose that
cancellation. This is a static sensitivity from supply steps of
$\pm10$~mV without noise; substrate coupling, unequal supply drops across
the die and locking between the rings are not covered.

\section{Cost and schedule of an academic shuttle}
\label{sec:shuttle}

Fabrication is more accessible than the size of the word suggests. The
University of Sevilla and IMSE-CNM are members of Europractice, whose
academic price list is public: a mini@sic block in UMC~180~nm costs
3\,430~\texteuro{}, one in TSMC~65~nm 3\,691~\texteuro{}, and IHP SG13G2 is
offered at 5\,110~\texteuro{}/mm$^2$~\cite{europractice_prices2026}; IHP
also announces free multi-project runs for open-source designs, without
publishing their area limits or conditions. The
engine is 0.35~mm$^2$ of cells without the capture buffer and
0.69~mm$^2$ with it, unrouted \simu{}; with the utilization range of
Section~\ref{sec:opt} the first figure becomes 0.50 to 0.59~mm$^2$ of
core \est{}. On IHP, which bills by area with a minimum equivalent to
0.8~mm$^2$, that is below the minimum, so the order would be the minimum
one. The mm$^2$ are those of this library: in the 180~nm alternative
the same 48.7~kGE, at the 9.677~$\mu\mathrm{m}^2$ per gate equivalent of
a published library of that
process~\cite{poschmann2009lightweight}, are 0.47~mm$^2$ of cells and
0.67 to 0.78~mm$^2$ of core \est{}, which fits about three times in the
2.33~mm$^2$ block; for 65~nm no gate area was collected, and no figure
is given beyond the 1~mm$^2$ minimum of that block. The chip would be
limited by its pads rather than by its logic. The
institute's own hardware-security group has already taken ring-oscillator
primitives from Xilinx~7-series FPGAs to characterized 65~nm silicon,
which makes the continuation of this work a matter of joining an
existing line rather than opening one.

What does not fit in a master's thesis is the schedule: from tape-out to
packaged samples takes months to over a year, the price does not include
packaging, and the characterization of the returned dies is a project in
itself. For comparison, a commercial secure element such as the
ATECC608B costs 0.90~\$ in single units and ships with a NIST entropy
source validation~\cite{microchip_atecc608b_ds}; two of its direct competitors
do not mention SP~800-90 anywhere in their datasheets. The honest
position of this work is therefore not to compete on price but on
\emph{transparency of the entropy}: every bit of a key produced by this
engine can be traced to a jitter that the device itself can measure
(measured in this work on the \ac{FPGA} prototype, simulated for the
integrated circuit) and to a stated bound, which is
precisely what a black-box part cannot offer.
